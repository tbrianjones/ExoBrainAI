# Summary and Brief Generation Framework

A comprehensive framework for creating effective summaries and briefs across all content types. Grounded in cognitive science, journalism, and SEO research.

## 1. Cognitive Foundations

### Working Memory Constraints

Miller's Law establishes that humans can hold approximately 4 chunks of information in working memory without rehearsal. Effective summaries respect this limit.

**Practical Implications**:
- Summaries should contain 3-5 key points maximum
- Chunking related information expands effective capacity
- Readers can allocate cognitive resources more effectively when summaries condense to core ideas
- Paraphrasing moves information to long-term memory

### How Readers Process Summaries

- Summaries reduce cognitive load by condensing to core ideas
- Readers monitor multiple aspects: protagonist, temporality, event relationships
- Prior knowledge significantly impacts ability to extract information
- Summarization fosters organization and integration of ideas

## 2. Summary Types and Specifications

### Article Summaries (Blog Posts, News, Features)

**Length**: 50-100 words (3-5 sentences); 40-60 words for featured snippet optimization

**Structure**:
- Place at top (inverted pyramid style)
- Use 5W1H method: Who, What, When, Where, Why, How
- Bulleted lists for technical content; narrative for story-driven content

**Include**: Main argument, key findings, essential data, the "so what"

**Exclude**: Examples, anecdotes, secondary details, background readers likely know

---

### Executive Summaries (Reports, Proposals, Business Documents)

**Length**: 1-2 pages; no more than 10% of original document

**Structure**:
1. Purpose: Document objective, problem addressed
2. Problem/Opportunity: The issue being solved
3. Solution/Recommendations: Proposed actions
4. Key Findings: Essential data supporting recommendations
5. Call to Action: Specific next steps

**Audience Tailoring**:
- Executives: 1-2 pages, strategic focus
- Investors: Vision and growth, 1-3 pages
- Lenders: Financial highlights, 2-3 pages

---

### Academic Abstracts

**Length**: 150-250 words (check journal requirements)

**Structure (IMRAD)**:
1. Introduction/Purpose: Research problem
2. Methods: Basic design
3. Results: Major findings with specific data
4. Discussion/Conclusions: Interpretations

**Types**:
- Informative: Purpose, methods, results, conclusions (most common)
- Descriptive: Purpose, scope, methodology only
- Critical: Adds evaluation and comparison

**Rules**: No citations, no information not in paper, write last

---

### Meta Descriptions (SEO Summaries)

**Length**: 150-160 characters; key information in first 100 characters

**Structure**:
- Lead with value proposition
- Include primary keyword naturally
- Add call-to-action
- Unique for every page

**Note**: Google rewrites 60-70% of meta descriptions. Focus on clarity and relevance.

---

### Social Media Summaries

| Platform | Visible Before "See More" | Optimal Length |
|----------|--------------------------|----------------|
| Twitter/X | Full post | 71-100 characters |
| LinkedIn | ~210 characters | ~100 characters |
| Instagram | 125 characters | Front-load hook |

**Structure**:
- Hook in first line
- Line breaks for readability
- End with engagement prompt
- 2-3 hashtags maximum

---

### Email Preheaders/Preview Text

**Length**: 40-100 characters; essential content in first 30 (mobile cutoff)

**Function**: Extension of subject line, not repetition

**Impact**: Emails with preheaders average 22.3% open rate vs. 19.3% without

---

### TL;DR Summaries

**Length**: 1-2 sentences; under 100 words maximum; no more than 4 points

**Structure**:
- Label clearly as "TL;DR"
- Single statement or brief list
- Place at end (traditional) or top (business)

**Style**: Casual, direct; memorable and repeatable; practical over profound

---

### Book/Chapter Summaries

**Length**: 5-10% of original content; 10-page chapter = 1-page summary

**Fiction Include**: Main character arc, major events, turning points, resolution

**Nonfiction Include**: Central thesis, key supporting points, critical insights, conclusions

**Rules**: Use your own words; no personal opinion; no examples or figures of speech

## 3. The Inverted Pyramid

The foundational structure for most summary writing.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│     Most Important (Lead)       │  ← Answers Who, What, When, Where, Why, How
├─────────────────────────────────┤
│     Supporting Details          │  ← Evidence, quotes, context
├─────────────────────────────────┤
│     Background/Lesser Details   │  ← Can be cut without losing essence
└─────────────────────────────────┘
```

**Benefits**:
- Readers can stop at any point and understand the main message
- Content can be "cut from the bottom" without losing essential information
- Reduces cognitive load by delivering answers upfront
- Well-suited for web and mobile consumption

## 4. Extractive vs. Abstractive Approaches

| Extractive | Abstractive |
|------------|-------------|
| Selects existing sentences | Generates new text |
| Like a highlighter | Like a pen |
| No hallucination risk | More human-like |
| Users rate as more informative | Users rate as more coherent |
| Best for: factual accuracy | Best for: marketing, comms |

**Best Practice**: Combine both; select key points (extractive thinking) and express them in new, clearer language (abstractive refinement).

---

## 5. Featured Snippet Optimization

For capturing Google's "Position Zero":

**Format**: 40-60 words for paragraph snippets (70% of snippets)

**Structure**:
- Question-based heading: "What is X?" or "How to Y?"
- Direct answer immediately following
- H2 or H3 subheadings framed as questions

**Requirements**: Page must already rank on first page; structure must match snippet format

## 7. Generation Process

### Step 1: Complete the Full Content First
Always write the summary last. You cannot summarize what does not exist.

### Step 2: Identify the Core Message
What is the single most important takeaway? If the reader remembers only one thing, what should it be?

### Step 3: Extract Key Points
Limit to 3-5 supporting ideas maximum. Apply working memory constraints.

### Step 4: Use Your Own Words
Paraphrasing demonstrates understanding and avoids plagiarism issues.

### Step 5: Front-Load Information
Most important content first. Inverted pyramid.

### Step 6: Eliminate Ruthlessly
Remove examples, anecdotes, qualifications, and anything that is not essential.

### Step 7: Test for Standalone Quality
The summary should make sense without the original. Would a reader unfamiliar with the source understand it?

### Step 8: Match Format to Context
Different channels require different approaches. Check length guidelines.

---

## 8. Quality Criteria

### Must Have
- **Complete**: Contains the essential message
- **Accurate**: Faithfully represents the source
- **Clear**: Meaning is immediately understood
- **Standalone**: Makes sense without the original

### Should Have
- **Concise**: Shortest possible while complete
- **Structured**: Information hierarchy is clear
- **Actionable**: Tells reader what matters

### Avoid
- Personal opinions (unless analysis)
- Verbatim copying
- Burying the lead
- Summarizing everything (focus on key points)
- Writing before completing the source

---

## 9. Common Mistakes

1. **Writing too much**: The most common error; summaries should be ruthlessly concise
2. **Writing before completing**: Always summarize last
3. **Including opinions**: Keep summaries objective unless analyzing
4. **Burying the lead**: Put the most important information first
5. **Copying verbatim**: Use your own words
6. **Summarizing everything**: Focus on key points, not completeness
7. **Ignoring audience**: Tailor length, tone, and content to readers
8. **Forgetting the "so what"**: Explain why it matters

---

## 10. Quality Check Questions

Before finalizing any summary, ask:

1. What is the single most important thing the reader must know?
2. If the reader stops after the first sentence, what do they take away?
3. What can be cut without losing essential meaning?
4. Is this clear to someone who has not read the original?
5. Does this respect the reader's time and cognitive limits?
6. Have I used my own words to express the ideas?
7. Does the length match the context and platform?
8. Would the reader prefer 3 strong points or 7 diluted ones?
