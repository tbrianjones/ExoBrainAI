# Trade Policy and Epistemic Humility

**Created**: 2026-01-11
**Status**: seed

## Summary

An exploration of what economics can actually tell us about trade policy, triggered by Howard Lutnick's confident claims on the All In podcast. The investigation reveals that economics is "worse than weather prediction" due to reflexivity; predictions change outcomes. But 150 years of trade history offers surprisingly robust patterns that recur across eras, even if precise forecasting remains impossible.

## Origin

Listening to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick discuss steel subsidies and tariffs on the All In podcast. The specific claims (Japan selling steel at $400/ton due to subsidies vs. US at $700/ton) sparked curiosity: how do you even evaluate claims like this? What's the framework? Is any of this knowable, or is it all motivated reasoning wrapped in economic language?

## Core Tension

The academic apparatus of economics provides vocabulary and constraints but not predictions. Yet policymakers must decide. The resolution: historical base rates and revealed preferences from 150 years of trade policy offer actionable heuristics, even without predictive certainty.

## Key Discoveries

1. **Tariff retaliation is near-certain and mutually harmful** (Smoot-Hawley, every trade war since)
2. **Trade imbalances are macroeconomic, not trade policy problems** (US-Japan 1980s proved this)
3. **Industrial policy works only with export discipline** (Asian Tigers vs. Latin America)
4. **Downstream industries pay upstream protection costs** (steel tariffs raise auto costs)
5. **Sanctions have 34% success rate**; work for modest goals, fail at regime change

## Open Questions

- How should an individual citizen evaluate trade policy claims from politicians?
- Is there a framework for distinguishing "motivated reasoning with equations" from genuine economic insight?
- What's the right level of epistemic humility for policymakers who must act under uncertainty?
- Can the "five findings" serve as a practical checklist for evaluating trade policy proposals?

## Planned Output

Blog post telling the story: curiosity → investigation → discovery that prediction is impossible → but history offers ground truth → five robust findings. Academic rigor with narrative flow.
