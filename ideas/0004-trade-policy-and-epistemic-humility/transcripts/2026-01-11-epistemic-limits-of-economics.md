### The Basic Trade Framework

The standard economic framing: when foreign governments subsidize their exports, there are winners (consumers getting cheaper goods, downstream industries getting cheaper inputs) and losers (domestic producers competing against artificially cheap imports). The "gift" argument emerged: if another country's taxpayers want to subsidize our consumption, isn't that their problem?

But complications pile up quickly: strategic dependency (what if you need that industry in a crisis?), industry clusters (steel workers, steel towns, steel suppliers), reciprocity (does accepting the gift encourage more predatory behavior?), and the impossibility of compensating losers cleanly.

### The Epistemological Turn: Is This All Tail-Chasing?

A direct question: "Is this all philosophical tail-chasing? Can we actually run math on a global economy, or is everyone just making up frameworks to justify what they already believe?"

The honest answer: economics is worse than weather prediction. Weather systems don't change because you predicted rain; economies do. If enough people believe a recession is coming, they stop spending, and the recession arrives. This reflexivity makes causal inference "brutally hard."

We can measure flows; exports, imports, prices, employment. These are accounting identities, constraints on what CAN happen. But causal questions? No control groups. Everything moves at once. Long causal chains with feedback loops. "The economy is a system where the observers are also participants who change the system by observing it."

### What Can Policymakers Actually Know?

If prediction is impossible, what grounds decisions? Five sources of knowledge:

1. **Accounting identities**: Current account deficit = capital account surplus. Trade deficit = (Investment - Savings). These aren't debatable; they're definitions.

2. **Historical base rates**: Not predictions, but priors. "When countries did X in the past, Y usually followed." Doesn't mean Y WILL follow this time, but the base rate is informative.

3. **Heuristics**: Rules of thumb that have survived many environments. "Don't impose tariffs in a recession" has a good track record.

4. **Revealed preferences**: What do countries actually DO, not what they SAY? Japan's behavior across decades reveals more than any speech.

5. **Optionality**: When uncertain, prefer policies that preserve future options over irreversible commitments.

### The Historical Report (Condensed)

The report covered major epochs:

**Tariffs**: The Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930 raised rates to 59.1%, triggered retaliation from 25 countries, and coincided with a 66% collapse in world trade. GATT/WTO liberalization brought tariffs from ~22% average to ~5% globally. Recent protectionism (Trump tariffs, EU responses) showed the pattern holds: tariff announcement followed by retaliation within weeks.

**Trade imbalances**: The US-Japan case of the 1980s proved that trade imbalances are macroeconomic, not trade policy problems. Voluntary Export Restraints, semiconductor agreements, and Plaza Accord currency intervention; all failed to fix the bilateral deficit because the deficit reflected savings/investment patterns, not trade rules.

**Industrial policy**: Asian Tigers (Korea, Taiwan, Singapore) succeeded with export discipline; subsidies tied to export performance. Latin American import substitution (Brazil, Argentina) failed; protection bred inefficiency. The difference wasn't having industrial policy; it was demanding that protected industries compete globally.

**Subsidies**: Steel subsidies average 10-20% of production costs in subsidizing countries. Downstream costs of protection: every $1 spent saving steel jobs costs $5-7 in downstream industries.

**Currency manipulation**: Plaza Accord (1985) depreciated the dollar 50% against yen and mark, yet the US trade deficit persisted. Currency moves don't translate cleanly into trade flows.

**Sanctions**: Meta-analysis of 200+ cases from 1914-2000 shows 34% success rate. Success strongly correlated with modest goals (policy change) vs. ambitious goals (regime change: ~10% success).

### The Five Hardest Findings

Distilling the historical record to five findings that recur across eras:

1. **Tariff retaliation is near-certain and mutually harmful.** Smoot-Hawley, every trade war since. The other side always retaliates. Both sides end up worse.

2. **Trade imbalances are macroeconomic, not trade policy problems.** The US-Japan case is definitive. Tariffs and quotas don't fix deficits caused by savings/investment gaps.

3. **Industrial policy works only with export discipline.** Asian Tigers vs. Latin America. Protection without competitive pressure breeds inefficiency.

4. **Downstream industries pay upstream protection costs.** Steel tariffs raise auto costs. The protected industry's jobs are visible; the downstream job losses are diffuse and politically invisible.

5. **Sanctions have 34% success rate; work for modest goals, fail at regime change.** Don't expect sanctions to topple governments. Expect them to sometimes change specific policies.
