# Critical Trends in International Trade Policy: A Historical Analysis (1870-Present)

## A Research Report for Policymakers

---

## Executive Summary

This report synthesizes 150 years of international trade policy history to identify patterns, lessons, and actionable principles for contemporary policymakers. The historical record reveals several consistent findings:

1. **Tariff escalation spirals cause mutual harm.** From Smoot-Hawley (1930) to the 2018 US-China trade war, retaliatory tariff cycles have consistently reduced trade volumes, raised consumer prices, and damaged employment; often in the very industries meant to be protected.

2. **Post-war trade liberalization generated unprecedented prosperity.** The GATT/WTO system reduced global average tariffs from 22% (1947) to under 5% (by 2000), coinciding with a fivefold increase in world trade between 1995 and 2023.

3. **Trade imbalances are primarily macroeconomic phenomena.** Tariffs are poorly suited to address trade deficits, which are driven by differences in national savings and investment rates.

4. **Industrial policy works only under specific conditions.** The Asian Tigers succeeded by combining targeted infant industry protection with aggressive export orientation; countries relying on pure import substitution (Latin America, Africa in the 1970s) generally failed.

5. **Subsidies distort trade more than tariffs.** The trade-distorting effects of subsidies are estimated at 2 to 4 times the equivalent tariff rates, and subsidy competition between major economies increasingly threatens the rules-based trading system.

6. **Currency manipulation and trade sanctions have mixed records.** The Plaza Accord (1985) successfully realigned exchange rates, while sanctions rarely achieve their stated political objectives; though they can impose significant economic costs.

## Part I: Global Tariff Trends (1870-Present)

### 1.1 The High Tariff Era (1870-1934)

From 1870 to the early 1930s, major economies maintained high tariff barriers. The United States was "the most protectionist country throughout history," with average tariff rates on imported manufactures ranging from 30% to 50% throughout the late 19th century. During this period, tariffs served two primary purposes: revenue generation (before income taxes became widespread) and protection of nascent industries.

**Key Statistics:**
- U.S. average tariffs on dutiable goods: 30-50% (1870-1913)
- U.S. share of global manufacturing: rose from 23% (1870) to 36% (1913)
- Estimated welfare cost of high tariffs: approximately 0.5% of GDP in the mid-1870s

The protectionist era culminated in two disastrous tariff acts:

**Fordney-McCumber Act (1922):** Raised U.S. average tariffs to 38%. Within five years, trading partners retaliated: France raised automobile tariffs from 45% to 100%, Spain increased tariffs on American goods by 40%.

**Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act (1930):** Raised duties on over 20,000 imports by an average of 20%, with agricultural tariffs increasing by 57%. The results were catastrophic:
- U.S. imports fell 66% from $4.4 billion (1929) to $1.5 billion (1933)
- U.S. exports fell 61% from $5.4 billion to $2.1 billion
- World trade declined 66% between 1929 and 1934
- Approximately 60 nations imposed retaliatory tariffs
- U.S. unemployment rose from 4.6% (1929) to 24.9% (1932)

**The Lesson:** Smoot-Hawley did not cause the Great Depression, but it worsened and prolonged it. Economic historians estimate the microeconomic distortion effects reduced U.S. GNP by approximately 2%. However, the larger damage came from the retaliatory spiral and collapse of international trade. Countries that protested or retaliated saw U.S. exports decline an additional 25%.

### 1.2 The Trade Liberalization Era (1934-2000)

The 1934 Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act marked a turning point, shifting U.S. policy toward negotiated tariff reductions. After World War II, the Bretton Woods system and the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) institutionalized multilateral trade liberalization.

**GATT/WTO Tariff Reductions:**
| Period | Average Tariff (GATT Participants) |
|--------|-----------------------------------|
| 1947 | 22% |
| 1962-67 (Kennedy Round) | 15% |
| Post-Uruguay Round (1994) | Under 5% |
| 2022 (trade-weighted) | 2.0% |

**Results of Liberalization:**
- The Uruguay Round reduced tariffs on industrial goods by an average of 40%
- Between 1995 and 2023, world trade grew at an average of 5.8% per year
- Total world trade increased nearly fivefold during the WTO era
- International trade costs decreased by 6-10% across various sectors (1995-2020)
- By 1968, countries in the Bretton Woods core accounted for 70% of global exports

### 1.3 The Return of Protectionism (2016-Present)

The post-2016 period has witnessed a reversal of liberalization trends.

**Key Statistics:**
- U.S. average tariff rate: 2.5% (2024) rising to 14.5-27% (early 2025)
- Global trade policy uncertainty in the 2020s: nearly triple the average level of the 2000s
- 70% of new trade restrictions (2022-24) were introduced by advanced economies

**The Pattern:** Tariff increases during the first wave of globalization (1870-1913) and interwar period showed fluctuations of 20 percentage points in a single year; similar in magnitude to recent changes. Historical analysis suggests these policy shifts were driven by political priorities rather than reactions to economic conditions.

## Part II: Trade Imbalances

### 2.1 The Macroeconomic Reality

Trade imbalances fundamentally reflect differences between national saving and investment rates. This is an accounting identity, not merely a theory. Countries that save more than they invest domestically will run trade surpluses; countries that invest more than they save will run deficits.

**Historical Data:**
- The U.S. last had a trade surplus in 1975
- China's current account surplus peaked at 9.9% of GDP in 2006
- Germany's trade surplus with the United States: approximately €69.8 billion (2024)
- U.S. overall trade deficit: nearly $1 trillion (2022)

### 2.2 The Failure of Tariffs to Correct Imbalances

The historical record is clear: tariffs are poorly suited to addressing trade imbalances.

**Case Study: U.S.-Japan Trade War (1980s)**

The United States ran a trade deficit with Japan reaching nearly $50 billion by the mid-1980s, losing 2 million manufacturing jobs between 1980 and 1985. The response included:
- Voluntary Export Restraints (VERs) on automobiles and steel
- The 1986 Semiconductor Agreement (mandating 20% U.S. market share in Japan)
- The Plaza Accord (1985) to depreciate the dollar

**Results:**
- The dollar fell 40% against the yen in 1985-87
- Japanese automakers adapted by building U.S. plants and shifting to higher-margin vehicles
- The U.S. trade deficit with Japan was not reversed even decades later
- American computer manufacturers faced higher costs and chip shortages due to semiconductor restrictions

**The Lesson:** While the Plaza Accord's exchange rate adjustment did improve the trade balance (with a lag), the sectoral protectionism produced mixed results. Protected industries gained temporary relief, but downstream industries using the protected inputs lost competitiveness. Japanese firms adapted strategically, often in ways that undermined the protectionist intent.

### 2.3 Consequences of Persistent Imbalances

Trade imbalances can have significant distributional effects even when aggregate welfare impacts are modest.

**The China Shock:**
- U.S. trade deficit with China eliminated an estimated 1.8 million U.S. jobs since 2001
- Manufacturing employment fell from 14.1% (1998) to 9.5% (2008) of the nonfarm sector
- Concentrated job losses hit "heartland" communities particularly hard

**Germany's Persistent Surplus:**
- Persistent imbalances within the eurozone led to financial imbalances and unbalanced growth
- Critics argued Germany should take steps to reduce its surplus to support eurozone recovery

**Recommended Approach:** The IMF's "targeting principle" suggests trade distortions should be addressed with trade policy, while macroeconomic imbalances require macroeconomic instruments (fiscal policy, exchange rate adjustment, structural reforms affecting savings behavior).

## Part III: Export-Led Growth and Industrial Policy

### 3.1 The Asian Tigers Model

Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan achieved extraordinary growth by averaging more than 7% GDP growth annually between 1960 and 1990. Their experience offers the clearest evidence on what makes industrial policy work.

**Key Statistics:**
- Average GDP growth: 7.5% per year for three decades
- South Korea: poverty fell from 40.9% (1965) to 4.6% (1984)
- Singapore: GDP growth reached 9.8% by 1990
- Foreign direct investment in trade regimes grew from 16% (1970) to 33% (1990)
- Share of exports in GDP tripled over 25 years

**What Made It Work:**

1. **Export orientation as the test:** Protection was conditional on export performance. Firms had to prove competitiveness in international markets, not just domestic ones.

2. **Realistic exchange rates:** The Asian Tigers did not allow exchange rate overvaluation, which would have taxed exports and subsidized imports.

3. **Access to inputs at world prices:** Exporters received duty exemptions and free trade zones ensuring access to imported inputs.

4. **Investment in human capital:** Universal primary education and rapidly expanding secondary education created a skilled workforce.

**The Contrast with Latin America:**
- Latin American countries pursuing pure import substitution without export requirements experienced stagnation in the 1980-2010 period
- Manufacturing growth rates dropped significantly when import substitution policies were maintained
- Protected firms never developed international competitiveness

### 3.2 The Infant Industry Argument: Mixed Evidence

The historical record on infant industry protection is mixed, with success depending critically on implementation.

**Failures:**
- **Turkey (1960s):** Protected sectors in non-electrical tools and paper did not become more efficient or competitive
- **Brazil (1980s computers):** The protected computer industry never matured; the technology gap with the rest of the world widened
- **British automobiles (1970s):** Policy backing "losers" resulted in continued decline
- **Africa and Latin America (1950s-1960s):** Firms protected from competition "largely failed to become competitive on the world market"

**Successes:**
- **South Korea and Taiwan:** Unlike most developing countries, Korean infant industries under protection tended to mature
- **Japan (semiconductors and computers):** Japanese industrial policy in high-tech sectors is generally considered successful
- **French cotton (Napoleonic era):** Regions protected from British imports by the blockade saw greater growth in mechanized cotton production

**Critical Success Factors:**
1. Protection must be temporary and conditional
2. Export performance must be required as a test of competitiveness
3. The infant industry argument works only for countries "not at the technology frontier"
4. Governments must be capable of "picking winners" and willing to withdraw support from failures

**Warning:** If policymakers select the wrong sector, "the infant industry will use the support to expand its capacity but will require continued protection for its survival because no real comparative advantage can be developed."

### 3.3 China's WTO Accession (2001)

China's entry into the WTO provides the most significant recent example of trade-driven transformation.

**Statistics:**
- Chinese export growth: 30% per year (2001-2006), more than double the previous five years
- China's share of world exports: 3.9% (2001) to 6.5% (2004)
- U.S.-China trade: $100 billion (1999) to $558 billion (2019)
- China's economy: 11 times larger than in 2001
- Chinese tariffs reduced from 15% (2000) to 9% (2006)

**Effects on the United States:**
- U.S. manufacturing price index reduced by 7.6% (2000-2006)
- Median industry prices fell 8% due to Chinese competition
- Manufacturing job losses: approximately 550,000
- Aggregate U.S. welfare increased by 0.2% long-run

**Effects on China:**
- Agricultural employment fell from 60% (1990) to 28% (2015)
- Total factor productivity for Chinese exporters grew 10% per year
- 69% of China's export growth came from new exporters offering widening product variety

**The Lesson:** Trade liberalization produced clear consumer benefits and aggregate welfare gains, but the costs were concentrated in specific communities and industries. This distributional pattern; aggregate gains but concentrated losses; appears consistently across trade liberalization episodes.

## Part IV: Subsidies and Their Effects

### 4.1 Scale of the Problem

Subsidies can be more distortive to trade than tariffs. The World Bank estimates that ad valorem equivalents of subsidy trade distortions are:
- Double the average tariffs for agricultural goods
- Quadruple the average tariffs for manufactured goods

### 4.2 Types of Trade-Distorting Subsidies

1. **Direct payments:** Cash transfers to producers
2. **Input subsidies:** Support for raw materials, energy, or other inputs
3. **Price supports:** Government intervention to stabilize or elevate market prices
4. **Export subsidies:** Financial incentives to boost international competitiveness

**Prohibited under WTO rules:** Export subsidies contingent on export performance and local-content subsidies.

### 4.3 Notable Subsidy Disputes

**Boeing-Airbus:** The longest-running trade dispute, with both companies alleging illegal state aid from the other side. This case illustrates how developed country subsidies can distort competition in high-value industries.

**Agricultural Subsidies:** Particularly damaging to developing countries, which are most vulnerable to trade-distorting effects. For low-income countries concentrated in agriculture, subsidy distortions are greatest precisely where they can least afford them.

### 4.4 The New Subsidy Competition (2020s)

Recent years have seen a significant escalation in industrial subsidies:
- U.S. Inflation Reduction Act (2022)
- EU Green Deal Industrial Plan
- Made in China 2025

**Concern:** This escalation has "contributed to a significant increase in global trade tensions" and raised fears of "subsidy wars." While subsidized exports from China were previously blamed for hurting Western industries, "these practices are increasingly being replicated elsewhere."

## Part V: Currency Manipulation and Exchange Rate Policy

### 5.1 The Plaza Accord (1985)

The most successful example of coordinated exchange rate intervention.

**Background:**
- The dollar surged more than 50% against major currencies in the early 1980s
- U.S. trade deficit reached 3.4% of GDP (1987)
- 2 million manufacturing jobs lost (1980-1985)

**The Accord:** France, West Germany, Japan, the UK, and the U.S. agreed to depreciate the dollar through coordinated intervention.

**Results:**
- The dollar fell 40% in 1985-87
- The yen appreciated from 240 to 120 to the dollar
- Trade balances improved (with the usual lag)

**Caution:** The adjustment exceeded intentions. "The original intention was for a dollar depreciation of 10-15 percent, not a halving in value against the yen." The Japanese economy experienced significant disruption, contributing to the asset bubble of the late 1980s.

### 5.2 China and Currency Manipulation

**Allegations:**
- China purchased over $2.2 trillion in foreign exchange reserves since 2001 to maintain an undervalued currency
- Estimates suggested the yuan was undervalued by more than 40% relative to the dollar
- This functioned as a 40% subsidy on Chinese exports and a 40% tax on U.S. imports

**China's Position:** China has rejected calls for a Plaza-style accord, having observed the adverse consequences for Japan.

**The Challenge:** Any contemporary exchange rate agreement would require Chinese participation to be effective, given China's role as one of the largest trading partners for the U.S., EU, Japan, and UK.

## Part VI: Trade Agreements

### 6.1 The Proliferation of Agreements

The World Trade Organization reports more than 300 active regional trade agreements worldwide, growing steadily since the early 1990s. These range from bilateral to regional to multilateral arrangements.

### 6.2 NAFTA/USMCA Case Study

The North American Free Trade Agreement (1994) was the most comprehensive free trade agreement of its time and served as a template for subsequent agreements.

**Assessment:**
- NAFTA "did not cause the huge job losses feared by the critics or the large economic gains predicted by supporters"
- Net effect on the U.S. economy was "relatively modest" because trade with Canada and Mexico represents a small percentage of U.S. GDP
- Worker and firm adjustment costs occurred as the three countries adapted to more open trade

### 6.3 Regional vs. Multilateral Trade Growth

Analysis of seven regional integration agreements (APEC, EU, NAFTA, ASEAN, CEFTA, MERCOSUR, Andean Community) from 1990-98 found:
- Imports from member countries increased 7% annually
- Imports from non-members increased 5.5% annually

This suggests regional agreements primarily create trade rather than simply diverting it; though both effects occur.

### 6.4 The "Spaghetti Bowl" Problem

The proliferation of bilateral and multilateral agreements has created what critics call a "spaghetti bowl" of crisscrossing arrangements with conflicting rules. This complexity can:
- Increase transaction costs for businesses
- Complicate multilateral negotiations
- Fragment the global trade landscape

## Part VII: Trade Sanctions

### 7.1 Overall Effectiveness

Analysis of over 200 sanctions imposed between World War I and the early 2000s suggests sanctions achieved "at least a modest contribution to a goal that was partly realized" only 34% of the time.

**Key Findings:**
- Multilateral sanctions work better than unilateral sanctions
- Sanctions by large countries against small ones work better than the reverse
- Sanctions demanding modest policy changes succeed more often than those aiming at regime change

### 7.2 Case Studies

**Cuba (1962-Present):** The most conspicuous example of failed unilateral sanctions. Over 60 years, the U.S. embargo "has not achieved any of its political goals and has served primarily to impoverish the Cuban people." Domestic interest groups (U.S. sugar manufacturers) developed vested interests in maintaining the embargo.

**Iran:** The 2015 nuclear deal represents a qualified success. Sanctions that "progressively constricted Iran's access to the international financial system" contributed to Iran accepting restrictions on its nuclear program. The economy contracted 9% over 2018-2019 under sanctions, though it recovered by 2021.

**Russia (2014-Present):** Sanctions after the Crimea annexation did not prevent the 2022 Ukraine invasion. However:
- Russia's GDP contracted 2.1% in 2022
- The country has relied heavily on sovereign wealth fund drawdowns
- Significant sanctions circumvention occurred through third countries (Armenia, UAE, Central Asia, Hong Kong)

**The Lesson:** "If the goal was to change Russian behavior with respect to Ukraine, the sanctions have failed. If their goal was to complicate the war effort and weaken the Russian economy, they have succeeded."

### 7.3 Circumvention and Costs

Sanctions can be circumvented through:
- Indigenous manufacturing of sanctioned goods
- Third-party trade with countries not in the sanctions coalition
- Development of alternative trade relationships

**Important:** Economic sanctions "generally inflict economic costs to all countries involved in the sanction episodes, including those taking the sanctions."

## Part VIII: Supply Chain Dynamics and Reshoring

### 8.1 The COVID-19 Shock

The pandemic highlighted vulnerabilities in global supply chains, accelerating trends already underway since the 2018 tariff increases.

**Current Status:**
- 86.2% of manufacturers surveyed have worked to de-risk supply chains in the past two years (Q4 2023)
- Global supply chain expansion has leveled off since the early 2010s

### 8.2 Geographic Reallocation

**Away from China:**
- China's share of U.S. trade declined more than 10% in 2024
- China's share of U.S. mobile phone imports fell from 64% to 42% (2024)

**Toward Alternative Locations:**
- Imports from Vietnam and India increased
- U.S. imports from Mexico increased 8%
- India's share of U.S. mobile phone imports rose from 0.15% (2015) to 10% (2024)
- FDI inflows to Mexico increased 11.9% in 2023, reaching $35.3 billion

### 8.3 Reshoring Trends

In 2024, U.S. manufacturing reshoring outpaced foreign direct investment by the largest margin since tracking began in 2010.

**Drivers:**
- Government incentives (top factor)
- Geopolitical tensions
- Rising Chinese labor costs
- Tariff avoidance
- Advanced manufacturing technologies (automation, robotics, AI)

**Constraints:**
- Higher North American labor costs
- Manufacturing labor shortages
- Infrastructure limitations
- Capital requirements for rebuilding domestic manufacturing

**The Reality:** U.S. firms are increasingly sourcing from Mexico, where labor costs are 20-30% lower than in China. This represents nearshoring rather than true reshoring.

## Part IX: Summary of Historical Lessons

### Patterns That Recur Across Eras

1. **Retaliation spirals harm all parties.** From Smoot-Hawley to the Chicken War to the 2018 US-China trade war, tit-for-tat tariff escalation consistently reduces trade volumes and welfare on all sides.

2. **Protected industries rarely mature without export discipline.** Pure import substitution has a poor track record; successful industrial policy combines protection with requirements for export competitiveness.

3. **Trade liberalization creates aggregate gains but concentrated losses.** The benefits of lower prices are diffuse; the costs of job displacement are geographically and sectorally concentrated. This creates persistent political economy challenges.

4. **Tariffs are poor tools for addressing trade imbalances.** Bilateral trade balances respond to macroeconomic fundamentals, not trade policy. The U.S.-Japan experience of the 1980s demonstrates this clearly.

5. **Downstream industries bear costs of upstream protection.** Protecting steel raises costs for automakers; protecting semiconductors raises costs for computer manufacturers. Protection is never cost-free.

6. **Exchange rate adjustment matters more than tariffs for trade balances.** The Plaza Accord achieved more in two years than decades of sectoral protectionism.

7. **Sanctions work best for modest objectives.** Regime change is rarely achieved; economic pressure for limited policy changes has a better track record.

## Part X: Actionable Principles for Policymakers

Based on the historical record, the following heuristics emerge:

### On Tariffs and Protection

1. **Avoid unilateral tariff escalation.** Historical evidence strongly suggests retaliation is likely, and mutual tariff increases reduce welfare for all parties.

2. **If protecting infant industries, require export performance.** The Asian Tiger model worked because protection was conditional and temporary, with international competitiveness as the test.

3. **Consider downstream effects.** Every protected industry is an input to other industries. Steel tariffs raise costs for automakers; semiconductor restrictions raise costs for computer manufacturers.

4. **Tariffs are inflationary and recessionary in the short run.** Empirical evidence shows tariffs raise prices and reduce economic activity, with at best modest positive effects on trade balances.

### On Trade Imbalances

5. **Address imbalances with macroeconomic tools.** Fiscal policy, savings incentives, and exchange rate policy are more appropriate instruments than trade policy for aggregate imbalance correction.

6. **Distinguish bilateral from aggregate imbalances.** Bilateral deficits with individual countries are largely meaningless; aggregate imbalances reflect domestic savings-investment gaps.

### On Industrial Policy

7. **Be realistic about government capacity to "pick winners."** The historical failure rate is high. If wrong choices are made, protected industries become permanently dependent on state assistance.

8. **Combine protection with competition.** Successful cases (Korea, Taiwan) maintained domestic competition and required export performance. Failures (Latin America) allowed protected firms to stagnate.

9. **Industrial policy works better for countries behind the technology frontier.** The infant industry argument has weaker justification for technologically advanced economies.

### On Subsidies

10. **Recognize that subsidy competition is collectively harmful.** The current escalation of industrial subsidies by major economies risks fragmenting the global trading system and reducing overall efficiency.

### On Sanctions

11. **Set modest, achievable objectives.** Sanctions work better for limited policy changes than regime change.

12. **Build multilateral coalitions.** Unilateral sanctions are more easily circumvented and less effective.

13. **Anticipate circumvention.** Third-party trade and indigenous production capacity will develop over time.

### On Supply Chains

14. **Diversification is more achievable than reshoring.** Moving supply chains from China to Vietnam, India, or Mexico is more economically viable than returning production to high-cost domestic markets.

15. **Advanced technology enables partial reshoring.** Automation and robotics change the labor-cost calculus, but significant capital investment is required.

## Contested Interpretations and Areas of Genuine Uncertainty

The historical record is not unanimous on several important questions:

1. **Did 19th-century U.S. protectionism contribute to industrialization?** Economic historians note that "economic growth is influenced by many factors other than trade policy" and that controlled comparisons are difficult. High tariffs coincided with rapid industrialization, but causation is not established.

2. **What is the optimal level of protection for developing countries?** The Asian Tigers combined elements of protection with export orientation, but replicating their success has proven difficult. The institutional capacity to implement similar policies appears to be a binding constraint.

3. **Are trade agreements net positive?** While evidence for the EU is consistently positive, results for other regional agreements are more mixed. The distributional effects of agreements remain contested.

4. **Can industrial policy succeed in advanced economies?** The historical evidence mostly concerns catch-up industrialization. Whether similar policies can work for economies at the technology frontier is less clear.

---

## Conclusion

The 150-year historical record on trade policy offers relatively clear lessons on what does not work: unilateral tariff escalation, pure import substitution without export discipline, using tariffs to correct macroeconomic imbalances, and economic sanctions aimed at regime change.

The record is more nuanced on what does work. Multilateral trade liberalization has coincided with unprecedented growth in trade and prosperity, but has also produced significant distributional challenges. Export-oriented industrial policy has succeeded in specific contexts (East Asia), but failed in others (Latin America, Africa). The conditions for successful intervention appear to include: temporary and conditional protection, export performance requirements, realistic exchange rates, and government capacity to withdraw support from unsuccessful industries.

The most robust finding is that trade policy is a poor substitute for macroeconomic policy. Trade imbalances reflect savings-investment gaps; exchange rates matter more than tariffs for trade balances; and the aggregate effects of trade restrictions are typically small relative to other economic forces.

For contemporary policymakers navigating renewed trade tensions, the historical record counsels caution about protectionist escalation, realism about the limits of trade policy in achieving macroeconomic objectives, and attention to the concentrated costs that trade adjustment imposes on specific communities and workers.
